01 / BACKGROUND

Predictive coding
and prediction error

Much of what reaches our senses is incomplete or ambiguous. A sound in a busy room, for example, could have several possible sources. To interpret it, we can draw on both the incoming information and what we already know about the situation.

Predictive coding is a proposed account of how the brain could perform this kind of inference. A model of the causes of sensory input generates predictions; differences between predicted and incoming signals help revise the interpretation. In hierarchical accounts, predictions pass from higher to lower levels, while mismatches help update higher-level representations.[1][2]

AN EVERYDAY EXAMPLE

Hearing a word in a noisy room

You are discussing a hot drink and expect to hear “tea”. That context helps interpret a muffled word. If the speaker repeats the word clearly and it is “key”, the new evidence challenges the original interpretation. An expectation can help make sense of uncertain input, while sufficiently reliable contradictory evidence can change it.

A prediction error is the mismatch between the expected signal and the signal received. It does not necessarily mean a conscious surprise or a mistake in reasoning. In a sensory model, the mismatch could concern a feature such as a sound's pitch or an edge's position.

Prediction

What the model expects to observe, given its current interpretation.

Incoming information

The signal that can support or challenge that expectation.

Prediction error

The difference used to revise the model's interpretation or expectations.

Why the reliability of evidence matters

A mismatch from a very noisy signal should not necessarily have the same influence as a mismatch from clear evidence. Predictive-coding accounts often describe this in terms of precision: the estimated reliability, or inverse uncertainty, of a signal. How much an expectation changes depends on the relative reliability assigned to the evidence and the prior expectation.[2]

A prior expectation is an expectation available before the current observation. Inference concerns the interpretation of what is happening now; learning changes expectations or model parameters through experience. These processes can operate at different timescales.

How this connects to our study

Our task concerns expected rewards rather than the interpretation of sensory input. We will use a reinforcement-learning model in which reward prediction errors change expected values. It shares the idea of updating from a mismatch, but it is a different, narrower model. Fitting it to choices does not directly test a cortical predictive-coding mechanism.

02 / COMPUTATIONAL PSYCHIATRY

Explaining behaviour
with a model

Imagine two people who earn the same number of points in a game. One changes their mind after almost every disappointing outcome. The other builds a sensible expectation, but does not always choose the option they think is best. Their final scores look similar. The processes behind those scores may be different.

Computational psychiatry uses explicit models of thinking and behaviour to investigate questions about mental health. A model is a small set of rules that generates predictions we can compare with actual behaviour.

“Computational” means that the explanation is precise enough to calculate and test. In this project, a simple learning rule lets us generate predictions for each decision and compare them with participants' choices.

03 / OUR RESEARCH QUESTION

Anxiety and learning
under uncertainty

Anxiety can involve worry about uncertain future events. One question for computational psychiatry is whether differences in anxiety symptoms are associated with differences in how people learn from uncertain outcomes.

There are at least two reasons for an unexpected outcome. It may be ordinary variability in an otherwise stable situation, or it may reflect a genuine change. In the task below, a good option sometimes produces no reward, and the option that pays more can also change.

NOISE

Outcomes vary.

A generally rewarding option can occasionally fail. One failure need not indicate a change in its underlying reward probability.

CHANGE

Probabilities change.

An option that used to pay more may become less rewarding. Older experience can then become less useful.

We will examine how strongly feedback changes a person's estimated reward values, and how consistently those values guide their next choice. The proposed symptom measure is the GAD-7, which asks about anxiety symptoms over the previous two weeks.[4]

RESEARCH QUESTIONAre anxiety symptoms associated with how people update reward expectations and use those expectations to choose?

We will analyse anxiety scores continuously across an adult sample. The focus is recent self-reported symptoms, rather than a diagnosis or a direct measure of stable trait anxiety.

What does previous research show?

Studies have linked anxiety to differences in learning under uncertainty. McCoy and Lawson (2025), for example, manipulated outcome noise and the frequency of reversals in a reward-learning task. Anxiety-related patterns differed across conditions and experiments.[5] Our simpler study will test an association without assuming that higher anxiety always means a higher or lower learning rate. Their study measured anxious traits; our proposed GAD-7 outcome measures recent symptoms.

The task is called probabilistic reversal learning: outcomes are uncertain, and the reward probabilities reverse. We observe choices consistent with learning and adapting expectations; the expectations themselves are inferred through the model.

05 / REWARD PREDICTION ERRORS

Calculating the difference
between expectation and outcome

An expectation is a guess about what will happen. In this task, it can be represented as a number between 0 and 1: an expected reward of 0.6 means expecting a one-point reward about 60% of the time.

A prediction error is the difference between what happened and what was expected. It is the information the model uses to update.

What happened−What I expected=Prediction error
  • A reward that was not strongly expected produces a positive prediction error.
  • A missing reward that was expected produces a negative prediction error.
  • An exactly expected outcome produces a prediction error of zero.

“Error” is a mismatch, not a mistake made by the participant. And a negative prediction error can mean no reward; the task does not need to deliver a punishment.

06 / THE LEARNING MODEL

Learning rate
and choice consistency

Our starting model is Rescorla–Wagner learning with a softmax choice rule. It keeps an expected reward value for each option, updates the chosen option after feedback, and uses the two values to generate a probability for the next choice.[3]

THE LEARNING RULE

New expectation = old expectation + α × prediction error

Qnew = Qold + α × (reward − Qold)

Q is simply the model's expected reward value. Because only the selected option's outcome is shown, the basic model leaves the unchosen option's value unchanged. We can start both values at 0.5: an equal initial expectation.

α
ALPHA / LEARNING RATE

How much should this outcome change my mind?

A lower α averages experience over a longer history. A higher α gives more influence to the latest outcome. Strong updating can be helpful when the world changes, but can also make the learner react to random disappointments while the world is stable.

There is no universally “good” learning rate. The useful balance depends on how noisy and changeable the environment is.

β
BETA / CHOICE CONSISTENCY

How strongly should my expectations guide my choice?

The model may value A more highly than B without choosing A every time. The softmax rule converts the value difference into choice probabilities. Its inverse-temperature parameter, β, controls how consistently the higher-valued option is selected.

Low β describes less consistent value-guided choices. It can have several explanations, including exploration or lapses of attention; the parameter alone cannot tell us which explanation is correct.

What does the softmax equation look like?
P(A) = 1 / [1 + exp(−β × (QA − QB))]

If the values are equal, the model assigns a 50% chance to each choice. When β is zero, choices are equally likely even if the values differ. With a larger positive β, a value difference produces a stronger preference.

Why start with this particular model?

It fits the task's immediate choice-and-feedback structure, has few parameters, and makes trial-by-trial predictions. We will treat it as a candidate explanation and check whether it captures the behaviour. A comparison model could add a tendency to repeat choices independently of reward. A fixed learning rate does not directly measure a belief about environmental volatility.[3]

Which task details still need to be finalised?

A working starting point is around 200–240 experimental trials, several unsignalled reversals, a short practice phase, and desktop/laptop participation. Trial count, reversal spacing, timings, task order, and total duration will be finalised after simulation and piloting. These are proposed design choices, not a validated off-the-shelf protocol.

We need outcomes that depend on the selected symbol, occasional unrewarded choices even for the better option, and a full trial-by-trial record. We will decide any compensation or performance bonus explicitly before the ethics submission.

What does the GAD-7 score represent?

The GAD-7 asks about the frequency of anxiety symptoms over the past two weeks. Higher summed scores indicate more frequent symptoms. We will retain the standard wording, response options, timeframe, and scoring.[4]

It is suitable for our question about recent symptoms, but it should not be described as a pure measure of stable trait anxiety. Some versions of the form include an additional question about functional difficulty; that question is not part of the seven-item total. We will obtain the standard form and scoring information when preparing the study materials.

08 / ANALYSIS

Relating learning parameters
to anxiety symptoms

Each participant provides two kinds of information: a sequence of task choices and outcomes, and questionnaire responses. The model connects the sequence of decisions to a small number of interpretable parameters.

  1. Describe behaviour.Check whether people learn which option is better, how often they switch after feedback, and how choices change around reversals.
  2. Fit candidate models.Replay each participant's actual history and find parameter values that make their choices more likely.
  3. Check the model.Simulate data with known parameters and check whether we can recover them. Also check whether the fitted model reproduces important patterns in actual behaviour.
  4. Test the symptom relationship.Use a pre-specified analysis to examine associations between learning parameters and GAD-7 total scores. Specify the primary test before examining these associations.
ASSOCIATION

Do the measures vary together?

Across our sample, do differences in learning parameters relate to differences in questionnaire scores? This is the primary ambition.

PREDICTION

Does the relationship generalise?

Can a model estimate scores for participants held out while it was developed? This requires separate validation; a significant association alone does not establish it.

AN ILLUSTRATIVE ASSOCIATION MODEL

Anxiety score = intercept + b1 × learning rate + b2 × choice consistency + residual

The coefficients b1 and b2 describe how GAD-7 scores vary with each parameter while accounting for the other. The residual is variation the model does not explain. This is a proposed analysis structure; the final model and sample-size justification will be agreed before recruitment.

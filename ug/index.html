01 / THE REASON FOR THE PROJECT

Similar symptoms.
Different responses.

People with depression can experience difficulties with mood, motivation, sleep and concentration, yet a similar clinical score does not tell us whether the same brain processes are involved in every person. This creates an important research question: could a physiological measurement taken before treatment help us understand variation in what happens afterwards?

Ketamine blocks the activity of NMDA-type glutamate receptors and can produce rapid antidepressant effects, as demonstrated in clinical trials, although an individual person's response varies.[1] Scopolamine blocks muscarinic acetylcholine receptors and has also been investigated for antidepressant effects, with mixed trial findings.[2] These receptors respond to different chemical messengers in the brain, so the two conditions provide a pharmacologically active comparison rather than a drug-versus-inert-placebo contrast.

The dataset lets us ask whether an EEG measure relates to outcome similarly or differently across the conditions. A drug's initial receptor action is only part of an explanation of its clinical effects, so a baseline EEG association would generate a hypothesis about relevant circuit properties rather than establish the complete mechanism of improvement.

OUR RESEARCH QUESTIONDo individual differences in visual gamma frequency or response strength before infusion predict improvement in depressive symptoms, and does that relationship depend on treatment condition or follow-up time?

Why look at the visual system?

A simple visual pattern gives us a controlled way to engage a brain circuit repeatedly and measure how it responds. Gamma oscillations offer a description of the circuit's dynamics, while a computational model can help us explore candidate processes that could generate those dynamics. The hypothesis is that this response may carry information about wider differences in circuit function that are relevant to treatment response.

The visual response is therefore a candidate physiological marker. Its relevance to this particular clinical outcome has to be tested. A relationship would not locate depression in visual cortex or establish that gamma itself causes recovery.

What is already known, and what is this project adding?

Human MEG studies show that ketamine can alter visual gamma activity.[3] Other work has linked aspects of resting gamma to antidepressant response in a more complex, baseline-dependent way.[4] Those findings motivate our question, but they involve different measurements or drug-related changes. Whether pre-infusion visual-grating EEG predicts later MADRS improvement in this dataset remains an open question.

03 / THE VISUAL GAMMA TASK

A pattern on the screen.
A response in the brain.

A visual grating is a pattern of alternating light and dark stripes. A typical trial starts with a fixation point, presents the grating for a period of time and then returns to the background. Repeating the trial helps us separate a consistent visual response from background activity and noise.[5]

The pattern can be stationary while the brain response oscillates. Gamma is the frequency of neural activity, measured in cycles per second, while the spacing of the stripes is the pattern's spatial frequency. These are different properties.

04 / READING A BRAIN RHYTHM

How fast?
How strong?

An oscillation is a repeating fluctuation in a signal. Gamma usually refers to relatively fast activity, often around 30–80 Hz in visual studies, although the relevant range and individual peak depend on the task and measurement.[5] At 50 Hz, one cycle lasts 20 milliseconds.

f

Peak frequency

The location of the gamma peak on the frequency axis, in hertz. A higher frequency means more cycles in the same amount of time.

A

Amplitude or power

Amplitude describes the size of a fluctuation. Power depends on its square, so doubling a clean oscillation's amplitude produces four times its power.

Where does the rhythm come from?

Neurons influence one another through connections called synapses, where chemical messengers act on receptors. Glutamate commonly supports excitatory signalling, which makes other cells more likely to become active, while GABA commonly supports inhibitory signalling, which restrains activity. Their effects unfold over time, creating the conditions for interacting populations to produce rhythms.

Interactions between excitatory and inhibitory neural populations can organise activity into repeated cycles. Excitatory activity recruits inhibition, inhibition restrains activity and the network can become active again as that influence decays. Real visual circuits contain additional cell types, connections and inputs, so this is an introductory account of one possible rhythm-generating mechanism.[6]

A SLOWED CONCEPTUAL ANIMATION

A teaching cartoon. The animation's speed is unrelated to actual gamma frequency.

Interpretation needs care. A gamma peak is an aggregate signal. Its height or frequency cannot be converted directly into “how much GABA” a person has or a single excitation-to-inhibition ratio. Stimulus properties, measurement quality and several circuit mechanisms can affect it.[7]

What exactly will “gamma amplitude” mean in our analysis?

We will agree a single operational definition. Options include peak amplitude, power in a specified frequency band or a stimulus-related power change relative to the pre-stimulus period. For power P, a common measure is 10 log₁₀(Pstimulus / Preference) in decibels, where 0 dB means equal power and a positive value means more power during the stimulus. Those values are not amplitudes in microvolts, and a power ratio should not be described as a raw voltage.

The spectral peak also sits on an aperiodic background, which generally falls with frequency. A peak-fitting approach can help distinguish a narrow oscillation from a broad change in the spectrum.[8] The chosen method must be suitable for these recordings and applied consistently.

05 / WHY “INDUCED” MATTERS

The response repeats.
The phase can differ.

A grating can produce a sustained increase in gamma activity during each trial even when the individual wave peaks do not occur at the same instant on every repetition. The response is related to the stimulus in its timing and strength, while its precise phase, the position within a cycle, can vary.[9]

When we average voltage traces first, positive and negative parts of these waves can cancel. When we estimate power within each trial and then average those estimates, the oscillatory response remains visible. This is why the way we average matters.

Is trial-averaged power automatically “induced power”?

Power averaged across individual trials is usually called total power and can contain both phase-locked and non-phase-locked activity. An evoked response has a consistent timing and phase across trials. Isolating the induced component may involve removing the trial-averaged evoked waveform before estimating power, or an equivalent decomposition, depending on the method.[9] We will specify what our pipeline actually estimates and avoid treating these labels as interchangeable.

06 / THE CLINICAL OUTCOME

What counts
as improvement?

The Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale, or MADRS, is a clinician-rated assessment of depressive symptom severity. Its ten items are scored from 0 to 6, giving a total from 0 to 60, with higher totals indicating greater severity.[10]

Improvement at a follow-up=Baseline MADRS−Follow-up MADRS

With this definition, a positive number means improvement, zero means no change and a negative number means worsening. A change from 32 to 20 gives a 12-point improvement. We will keep this direction explicit in every plot and table.

Several follow-ups help us distinguish early change from a more sustained improvement. Someone may improve quickly and then lose some of that benefit, while another person may follow a different trajectory. Repeated scores from the same person remain related observations.

Why account for the starting MADRS score?

A person starting with a higher score has more room for an absolute reduction, and baseline measurement error also enters the change score. We will therefore account for starting severity when evaluating a gamma–outcome association. One suitable approach at a chosen follow-up is to model the follow-up score while including baseline MADRS as a covariate.[11]

The explorer also shows percentage improvement for intuition. Percentage change divides by baseline and is undefined when baseline is zero. It does not automatically solve the statistical problems associated with baseline differences.

07 / FROM MEASUREMENT TO RESEARCH QUESTION

Does the rhythm
tell us anything useful?

For each usable participant or pre-infusion session, we will have an EEG feature and a subsequent clinical outcome. We can ask whether people with different baseline gamma frequencies or response strengths also differ in their later improvement. The direction of any relationship is an empirical question, so faster or stronger gamma is not automatically “better”.

GENERAL ASSOCIATION

A marker of outcome

If a similar association appears in both conditions, gamma may relate to improvement across those conditions. This could motivate a broader prognostic hypothesis.

TREATMENT INTERACTION

A difference between conditions

If the gamma–outcome relationship differs by condition, gamma may help explain variation in the relative response. This requires testing the difference between the relationships directly.

A statistically significant association in one condition and a non-significant association in the other do not, by themselves, show that the associations differ. The relevant test is a gamma × treatment interaction, supported by an estimate and its uncertainty.

What would a simple statistical model look like?
Follow-up MADRS = intercept + baseline MADRS term + gamma term + treatment term + gamma × treatment term + residual

Each term describes a contribution to the expected follow-up score. The interaction asks whether the gamma slope differs between treatment conditions. Lower predicted follow-up MADRS means a better outcome. Because the outcome here is a score rather than an improvement value, the sign of the gamma slope has a different interpretation from the explorer above.

This is a teaching model structure. Whether we can estimate all of these terms reliably depends on the sample. With repeated sessions or several follow-ups, the model must also account for observations belonging to the same person, with time and session structure specified from the protocol.

↗

Association is a first result. Prediction needs validation.A relationship fitted to this dataset does not establish that it will forecast outcomes for a new patient. That requires evaluation on observations held out from model development, with all sessions from a person kept together.[12]

A null or imprecise result can also be informative. It may constrain the proposed relationship, but small samples, unreliable gamma estimates or wide confidence intervals can leave substantial uncertainty. We should report what the data can support.

08 / OPTIONAL MODELLING EXTENSION

From a peak
to a possible mechanism.

Frequency and power summarise the measured response. A thalamocortical model, or TCM, offers another level of explanation by describing interacting neural populations and generating a predicted spectrum from their properties. The model developed by Shaw and colleagues contains six cortical and two thalamic populations, and was used to investigate ketamine-related changes in healthy-volunteer MEG.[13]

For this project, an optional extension would fit a suitable model to pre-infusion spectra and ask whether a small, justified set of parameter estimates relates to outcome. This would investigate a candidate circuit explanation for individual differences, rather than measuring receptors directly. We would first need to establish that the EEG data and observation model support reliable fitting.

What extra work would a real TCM analysis need?

It would need appropriate spectral inputs, a justified observation model, sensible priors and checks on fit quality, parameter recovery and uncertainty. Fitting only gamma peak frequency and height would give very limited information for a model with many parameters. Similar spectra can arise from different parameter combinations, so we would restrict the questions and compare plausible explanations rather than interpret every fitted parameter.

The published TCM motivates this extension, but moving from that MEG application to these EEG data requires validation. It is an optional extension after the main gamma analysis is working.

09 / WHAT YOU WILL ACTUALLY DO

A clear analysis
from signal to inference.

  1. 01

    Understand the dataset

    Read the protocol and data dictionary, map study IDs to sessions and treatment conditions, and check which MADRS follow-ups are available. Define the usable sample separately from the number of EEG trials.

  2. 02

    Check the EEG

    Inspect channels, events and trial timing, then review signal quality and the effects of preprocessing. Muscle activity, eye movements and line noise can affect the high-frequency signal, including around the gamma band.[14]

  3. 03

    Estimate the visual response

    Use a justified electrode region or source, a defined sustained-response window and a pre-stimulus reference. Examine time–frequency plots and spectra, estimate gamma frequency and the agreed strength measure, and record uncertainty and trial counts.

  4. 04

    Link EEG to the clinical trajectory

    Join measurements using study IDs, calculate clearly signed improvement values and plot individual trajectories. Report missing observations and inspect whether missingness differs by condition or starting severity.

  5. 05

    Test a focused primary question

    Agree one primary gamma feature and one protocol-led follow-up before examining outcome relationships, account for baseline severity and report effect sizes with confidence intervals. Treat additional features, timepoints and interactions according to a pre-specified exploratory or multiplicity plan.

  6. 06

    Evaluate what the result means

    Check sensitivity to influential observations and defensible analysis choices. Distinguish the observed association from a validated clinical predictor, then consider whether a carefully scoped TCM extension is feasible.

A gamma frequency estimate needs a reliable peak. Some recordings may not contain a reliable narrowband peak. We will define detection and quality rules without looking at MADRS outcome, record exclusions and describe who remains in the analysis.
How will we keep the analysis proportionate to the sample?

The number of people constrains an analysis of individual differences, even if each person completed many EEG trials. We will begin with simple, justified models and avoid dividing participants into arbitrary “high gamma” and “low gamma” groups. Interactions and prediction models may need more data than the core association analysis.

Repeated measurements require an analysis that respects participant identity. If prediction is attempted, preprocessing decisions learned from the data and feature selection must be made within the training data, with all sessions from each person assigned to the same validation fold.[12]

WHAT WILL WE LEARN?

Whether a controlled, pre-treatment brain response carries information about later symptom improvement, how that evidence depends on the treatment and timepoint, and how far a measured brain rhythm can take us towards an explanation of individual differences.

10 / FOLLOW THE EVIDENCE

Reading, with
a reason to read it.

Start with the visual gamma paper and the clinical rating scale, then move to the modelling paper when the main analysis is clear. The published studies below provide background; they should not be assumed to describe the dataset used in this student project.

  1. 1 / CLINICAL MOTIVATIONZarate et al. (2006). A randomized trial of an N-methyl-D-aspartate antagonist in treatment-resistant major depression.

    Evidence for rapid antidepressant effects of ketamine in a controlled clinical study.

  2. 2 / THE ACTIVE COMPARISONPark et al. (2019). Neurophysiological changes associated with antidepressant response to ketamine not observed in a negative trial of scopolamine in major depressive disorder.

    Why scopolamine has its own pharmacology, and why antidepressant benefit should not be assumed. Compare these negative findings with the earlier positive trial by Furey & Drevets (2006), Antidepressant efficacy of the antimuscarinic drug scopolamine: a randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial.

  3. 3 / GAMMA AND KETAMINEShaw et al. (2015). Ketamine amplifies induced gamma frequency oscillations in the human cerebral cortex.

    A human MEG study of drug-related changes in visual and motor gamma.

  4. 4 / GAMMA AND CLINICAL RESPONSENugent et al. (2019). Ketamine has distinct electrophysiological and behavioral effects in depressed and healthy subjects.

    A more complex relationship between baseline gamma, post-ketamine gamma and outcome, using resting MEG.

  5. 5 / THE VISUAL RESPONSESwettenham, Muthukumaraswamy & Singh (2009). Spectral properties of induced and evoked gamma oscillations in human early visual cortex to moving and stationary stimuli.

    How visual stimuli engage gamma oscillations, with distinctions between components of the response.

  6. 6 / CIRCUIT MECHANISMSCardin et al. (2009). Driving fast-spiking cells induces gamma rhythm and controls sensory responses.

    An experimental manipulation linking inhibitory cells to gamma rhythms in an animal preparation.

  7. 7 / AVOIDING A SIMPLE BIOCHEMICAL READOUTCousijn et al. (2014). Resting GABA and glutamate concentrations do not predict visual gamma frequency or amplitude.

    Evidence against treating visual gamma as a straightforward measure of neurotransmitter concentration.

  8. 8 / MEASURING A SPECTRAL PEAKDonoghue et al. (2020). Parameterizing neural power spectra into periodic and aperiodic components.

    Why a narrow oscillatory peak and the broader spectral background need to be distinguished.

  9. 9 / INDUCED AND EVOKED ACTIVITYDavid, Kilner & Friston (2006). Mechanisms of evoked and induced responses in MEG/EEG.

    The relationship between stimulus locking, phase and power.

  10. 10 / MEASURING SYMPTOM CHANGEMontgomery & Åsberg (1979). A new depression scale designed to be sensitive to change.

    The original MADRS paper. See also the rating form and scoring guidance.

  11. 11 / STARTING SEVERITY AND FOLLOW-UPVickers & Altman (2001). Analysing controlled trials with baseline and follow up measurements.

    Why the baseline value matters when analysing a later outcome.

  12. 12 / ASSOCIATION AND GENERALISATIONVaroquaux et al. (2017). Assessing and tuning brain decoders: cross-validation, caveats, and guidelines.

    How to evaluate a model without letting information from held-out observations leak into its development.

  13. 13 / THE OPTIONAL MODELLING EXTENSIONShaw et al. (2020). Generative modelling of the thalamo-cortical circuit mechanisms underlying the neurophysiological effects of ketamine.

    The eight-population TCM and its application to broadband MEG spectra.

  14. 14 / EEG QUALITYWhitham et al. (2007). Scalp electrical recording during paralysis: quantitative evidence that EEG frequencies above 20 Hz are contaminated by EMG.

    Experimental evidence for the need to distinguish muscle activity from high-frequency neural signals. For eye-movement contamination, see Yuval-Greenberg et al. (2008), Transient induced gamma-band response in EEG as a manifestation of miniature saccades. These artefacts do not imply that every measured gamma response is non-neural.

A short glossary
EEG
Electroencephalography, the recording of voltage differences at the scalp.
Gamma
A high-frequency range of brain activity, with the exact analysis range specified for the study.
Hertz, Hz
Cycles per second.
Spectrum
A description of how signal strength is distributed across frequencies.
Phase
Position within an oscillatory cycle.
Covariate
A variable included in an analysis to account for a relevant source of variation.
Interaction
A relationship that changes depending on another variable, such as treatment condition.
Generative model
A specified mechanism that produces predicted observations for comparison with measured data.
